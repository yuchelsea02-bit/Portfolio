Explore Opportunities

Starting need statement, stress-tested for scope before we commit to it.

Initial need statement

"I want an app that helps UBC students know how busy the gym is and whether the equipment they want to use is available."

Ways to sharpen it
  1. Too specific: it doesn't have to be an "app" — a lobby screen, a website, or signage at the door could all work. Locking in "app" rules out solutions before we've compared them.
  2. Too general: UBC has several rec facilities (BirdCoop, the Nest Fit Centre, Osborne) of very different sizes — "the gym" is ambiguous, and the right design goals may depend on which one.
  3. Unclear: "how busy" quietly bundles two different questions — overall crowd level, and whether one specific machine is free — that a design might need to answer differently.
Rephrased need statement

"I want something that helps UBC students understand current gym crowd levels and equipment availability so they can choose a better time to work out."

02

Define the Problem

Three considerations, promoted to design goals we can actually score solutions against.

Goal 1

Accuracy

Description

The design should report crowd and equipment status that reflects real, current conditions at the gym.

Justification

An inaccurate design is worse than no design — a student who trusts false "free" status wastes a trip they could have planned around.

Goal 2

Timeliness

Description

The design should refresh status often enough that it reflects the gym right now, not conditions from an hour ago.

Justification

Without being timely, a design is worse because stale data is functionally the same as no data — the student is back to guessing.

Goal 3

Privacy

Description

The design should report occupancy and equipment use without exposing who, specifically, is at the gym or what they're doing.

Justification

A private design is good because students should be able to trust the system without feeling watched while they work out.

Accessible — dropped, because even if checking the status takes an extra tap, that's a minor cost next to whether the number on screen can actually be trusted.

Feasible — dropped, because at this ideation stage we want to compare what the best possible design looks like before letting build cost narrow the field.

03

Brainstorm Approaches

Three genuinely different mechanisms for answering "is it busy, and is my machine free?"

1 Crowdsourced check-in app

Students tap "in" when they arrive and "out" when they leave, tagging which machine or zone they're using. The app aggregates live check-ins into a headcount and a list of equipment currently claimed.

2 Sensor-based equipment tracker

Pressure mats or Bluetooth beacons on each machine automatically detect use and feed a public dashboard showing exactly which equipment is free, no student action required.

3 Computer-vision occupancy counter

Entrance cameras count people and estimate density per zone (e.g. "weight room: quiet"), shown on a lobby screen and mirrored in the UBC Rec app — no per-machine detail, only area-level crowding.

Recommended solution — Sensor-based tracker

The sensor tracker wins because it scores well on all three goals at once: it's automatic, so it doesn't inherit the check-in app's dependence on inconsistent human behaviour, and it sidesteps the camera's privacy problem entirely. The check-in app is the cheapest to build, but its accuracy is capped by whether students actually remember to log in and out — which undercuts the entire point of a "trustworthy" status. The camera system is arguably the most complete picture at a zone level, but filming a space where people are self-conscious about their bodies carries an ethical cost the table can't fully capture, on top of being the most expensive to install. So even though no single solution sweeps every cell, the sensor approach is the one that doesn't trade one goal away to win another.

Where intuition and goals disagreed

Our first instinct actually leaned toward the camera system — it felt the most "complete" — but privacy wasn't weighted heavily enough at the goal-setting stage given how self-conscious people are about being watched at the gym. That gap suggests we should have imagined the student's perspective in the gym itself before finalizing goals, rather than picking considerations that sounded good in the abstract.
